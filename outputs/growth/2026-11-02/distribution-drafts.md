## LinkedIn: Make vs buy analysis starts with the same part

At 100 parts, buying can be cheaper. At 500, making can be cheaper. The reason may be fixed cost, not a different machine rate.

Here is a simple illustrative example:

- Make: $2,000 setup and fixtures, plus $12 per part.
- Buy: $200 one-time charges, plus $18 landed per part.
- At 100 parts: $3,200 to make, $2,000 to buy.
- At 300: both cost $5,600.
- At 500: $8,000 to make, $9,200 to buy.

The comparison only works if both routes deliver the same revision, material, finishing and inspection scope. It also assumes the in-house route can actually make the part and has the capacity.

## Community answer: a STEP file opening does not mean it is manufacturable

Check two things separately: whether the imported file represents the intended geometry, and whether a named manufacturing process can make that geometry under the actual drawing requirements.

I would first compare units, a known dimension, body count and revision with the source CAD. Then review tool access, setups, workholding and the drawing requirements for the intended process. Keep missing material or tolerance information as an open item rather than treating it as a pass.

NIST’s STEP analyzer is useful for file structure and PMI checks. For manufacturing review, use a process-specific check and inspect the affected feature, not just a score.

## Original research protocol

Question: Which missing inputs most often prevent a useful in-house manufacturing decision?

Use permissioned real cases with a defined period and inclusion rule. Record process, missing input category, whether the input was later supplied, whether the decision changed, and actual-job evidence when available. Keep company/part identifiers private. Publish aggregate counts with the denominator, exclusions and limitations. Do not turn the existing sample fixture, synthetic parts or repeated runs of one part into a customer dataset. A journalist quote should come from the actual findings after analysis, not the protocol.
